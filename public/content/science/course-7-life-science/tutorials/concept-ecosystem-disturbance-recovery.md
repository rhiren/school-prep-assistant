# Ecosystem Disturbance and Recovery

## Hook

A storm knocks down woodland trees. Sunlight reaches the ground, small plants spread, and the patch looks green again. Yet birds that nested in old tree cavities remain scarce.

Has the ecosystem recovered? Make a prediction before reading: what would you measure besides greenness?

## Big Idea

A disturbance changes conditions or organisms in an ecosystem. Its effects travel through resources, habitat, and food-web connections. Recovery can happen at different rates for different species and functions.

**Ask what recovered, compared with what, and over how long.**

## Learn It

### What remains matters

After a disturbance, soil, roots, surviving organisms, and nearby sources of plants may remain. These can support regrowth. Newly exposed bare rock has a very different starting point from a damaged woodland that still has soil.

Community composition can change over time as organisms establish and alter conditions. This is ecological succession. It does not mean one plant changes into another species, and it does not guarantee one fixed final community.

### Follow direct and indirect effects

Tree loss directly changes shade. That can affect seedlings, but water and soil still matter. Loss of host plants may reduce insects, which may later reduce food for birds. The bird response is indirect and may be delayed.

### Track conditions through time

Repeated trampling, drought, or mowing can interrupt regrowth. If conditions remain different, the later community may differ from the original one. Recovery need not follow a smooth upward line.

## Build The Model

`storm -> canopy loss -> more ground light -> changed seedling community`

Now add possible constraints: water availability, soil condition, and plants available to establish.

A second route is:

`host-plant loss -> fewer insects -> less food for insect-eating birds`

The second route predicts an indirect effect. It does not predict an exact number of birds without data.

## Memory Anchors

- **What remains shapes what returns.**
- **Green is one measure, not the whole habitat.**
- **Compare before, after, and a reference.**

## Evidence Rules

A baseline describes the site before the disturbance. A similar reference site helps identify shared changes, such as a rainy season.

| Site | Before | Just after storm | One year later |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A: plant count | 100 | 20 | 80 |
| B: plant count | 100 | 70 | 75 |

B retained more plants initially. A gained more afterward: 60 compared with 5. Neither returned to 100. These observations answer different questions; choosing only the final value misses the story.

Use the same sampled area and observation time. Measure several indicators when possible: plant cover, species identities, soil erosion, and habitat use. A rise in total cover does not establish that every original species returned.

## Common Traps

- "It is green, so it is fully restored." One species can provide lots of cover while other habitat features remain absent.
- "Recovery always brings back exactly the old community." Changed conditions can support different organisms.
- "A change after treatment proves treatment caused it." Weather and other shared influences also need consideration.
- "Recovery must improve every month." New disturbances and seasonal changes can alter the trend.

## Apply It

A closed trail edge gains 25 plants; a matched open edge gains 20 during the same rainy period. Both began with 20 plants.

The closure site improved more, but only by 5 plants relative to the comparison. It would be too strong to assign all 25 plants to closure. Repeated sites and longer observation would make the conclusion more reliable.

## Try At Home

**Observation study: recovery after routine mowing.** Spend 10 minutes once a week for three weeks. Use a notebook, a camera if available, and an adult-approved viewing spot beside a lawn that has already been mowed.

1. Predict how visible height and cover will change.
2. Choose one fixed small patch visible from the edge. Photograph it from the same place each visit.
3. Record visible cover, approximate height, recent rain, and any further mowing. Use consistent descriptions if measuring would require entering the patch.
4. If available, observe a nearby unmown patch at the same times and record differences in shade and watering.

Do not mow, remove plants, or create a disturbance for this activity. Stay away from machinery, roads, treated lawns, and unfamiliar plants.

**Explain:** Did repeated mowing change the trend? What remained from the original lawn? These observations show short-term regrowth. They do not prove complete ecological succession or recovery of all species. Nearby patches may also differ in water or light, so describe them as comparisons with limitations.
