# Systems of Equations Word Problems

## Two unknowns need two useful relationships

A situation may provide a count and a cost, two different prices, or two moving quantities. Define each variable with its unit before translating those relationships. The two equations must describe the same situation and the same variable meanings.

## Tickets: count versus revenue

A show sells 20 tickets. Adult tickets cost $8, child tickets cost $5, and revenue is $130.

Let a be the adult ticket count and c be the child ticket count.

`a + c = 20`

`8a + 5c = 130`

Multiply the count equation by 5: `5a + 5c = 100`. Subtract it from the revenue equation: `3a = 30`. Thus `a = 10` and `c = 10`.

Check both facts: 10 + 10 gives 20 tickets, and `8(10) + 5(10)` gives $130. A correct count alone would not verify the revenue.

## Comparing plans over time

Plan A costs $12 plus $2 per visit. Plan B costs $5 per visit. Equal cost means `12 + 2v = 5v`, so `v = 4`. At four visits, both cost $20. After four visits, A becomes cheaper because each extra visit adds $2 rather than $5.

The initial fee tells you the starting amount; it is not the rate.

## Check whether the result is possible

Ticket counts must be whole and nonnegative. If a reported revenue produces `a = 1/3`, do not round it to force an answer. The report is inconsistent with whole tickets. In contrast, a mixture measured in kilograms can legitimately have fractional amounts.

## Detect missing information

If all 20 tickets cost $5, knowing both 20 tickets and $100 revenue does not tell you how many were adult or child tickets. The revenue equation is just five times the count equation. You need another independent fact.

## Practice explaining the model

For bicycles b and tricycles t, `b + t = 12` counts vehicles and `2b + 3t = 28` counts wheels. Subtract twice the first equation to find `t = 4`; then `b = 8`. Describe the answer as eight bicycles and four tricycles, not just two unlabeled numbers.
