# Competition and Resource Partitioning

## Hook

Two seedlings grow in the same pot. Both receive sunlight, but the soil dries quickly and each grows less than a seedling grown alone.

Did they compete for water? Perhaps. They might also have shaded each other or shared too few minerals. Start with a prediction: what would you measure to distinguish these explanations?

## Big Idea

Competition can occur when organisms use the same limited resource and one organism's use reduces what is available to another. It can happen within a species or between species, with or without direct contact.

Resource partitioning means using different portions of resources, such as different foods, locations, or feeding times. It can reduce overlap and help organisms coexist. It does not require an agreement between them.

## Learn It

### Identify the shared resource

Plants can compete for light, water, mineral nutrients, and growing space. Animals may compete for food, shelter, or other limited requirements. Two organisms living nearby do not automatically compete strongly: ask whether they share a resource and whether its supply matters.

A tall plant may reduce light reaching a smaller plant. Roots can use water before neighboring roots reach it. Neither interaction requires a visible fight.

### Look for differences in use

Two birds may feed at different heights. Two plants may obtain much of their water from different soil depths. Different food sizes can also reduce overlap.

Timing matters only in context. If a morning visitor empties a food supply that does not renew before evening, the later visitor can still be affected.

### Keep more than one resource in mind

Species with different diets can still compete for scarce shelter. Extra water may reduce plant competition while leaving light or minerals limiting. Partitioning can support coexistence without guaranteeing it under all future conditions.

## Build The Model

`neighbor roots take up water -> less water available -> reduced growth of the focal plant`

Test the arrows rather than only labeling the interaction.

| Growing condition | Alone: growth | With neighbors: growth | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Low water | 7 cm | 3 cm | 4 cm |
| High water | 7 cm | 6 cm | 1 cm |

Extra water reduces the gap from 4 cm to 1 cm. This supports water as a contributor, but it does not show that water explains every difference.

A partitioning model might show:

`deep roots -> deeper water`

`shallow roots -> water near the surface`

These routes can still overlap, and drought can change which water is available.

## Memory Anchors

- **Shared plus limited can mean competition.**
- **Different use can reduce overlap.**
- **A smaller gap is not always a complete explanation.**

## Evidence Rules

Compare similar organisms under similar conditions. To investigate a neighbor effect, measure growth with and without neighbors while keeping pot size, light, time, and other relevant conditions comparable.

Record the proposed resource too. A growth difference alone does not tell you whether water, light, or minerals caused it. Repeat comparisons to check consistency.

Distinguish per-individual growth from a group's total. Ten small seedlings may have more combined mass than one large seedling, even if crowding reduces each individual's growth.

## Common Traps

- "Competition means fighting." Resource depletion can affect organisms that never meet.
- "Same habitat means same niche." Organisms in one place can use different resources. A niche includes resource use and interactions, not just location.
- "Different foods remove all competition." Other shared requirements may remain.
- "Partitioning is a fairness agreement." It is an observed pattern, not evidence of conscious negotiation.

## Apply It

A field survey finds fewer small plants beneath dense shrubs. A student blames competition for minerals.

That is a hypothesis. Shade, water use, soil differences, and other conditions could also matter. Measure the proposed pathway and compare similar sites before assigning the entire pattern to one cause.

## Try At Home

**Paper model: sharing a limited supply.** Allow 15 minutes. Use a sheet of paper, pencil, and 20 large paper squares labeled "food." This models a finite supply; it is not a study of real feeding behavior.

1. Predict what will remain for collector B if collector A takes 12 of 20 squares first.
2. Count and record the result. Reset to 20; let A take 15 this time. Record B's maximum possible remainder.
3. Reset again. Divide the paper into two resource patches of 10 squares. Let A take 8 only from one patch and B take 8 only from the other.
4. Compare the shared-supply and separated-patch models.

| Model | Starting supply | A takes | Maximum available to B |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Shared, first run | 20 | 12 | |
| Shared, second run | 20 | 15 | |
| Separate patches | 10 each | 8 from A's patch | |

Use paper squares large enough to handle easily. Do not move, feed, or collect living organisms.

**Explain:** Which rule changed the result? The model demonstrates finite supply and reduced overlap. Real organisms do not necessarily take turns, have identical needs, or stay in assigned patches. It cannot prove competition in a real habitat.
