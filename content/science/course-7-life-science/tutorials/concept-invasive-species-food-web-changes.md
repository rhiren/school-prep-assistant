# Invasive Species and Food-Web Changes

## Hook

A lake gains an introduced fish. A native predator begins eating it, but native fish that share its food grow more slowly.

Has the newcomer helped or harmed the ecosystem? One observation may not settle the question. Predict which organisms you would monitor and which feeding links could explain their responses.

## Big Idea

A non-native species occurs outside its natural range through introduction. Calling it invasive also involves evidence that its introduction causes or is likely to cause harm. Non-native does not automatically mean invasive.

An introduced species can affect an ecosystem through predation, competition, or changes to habitat. Effects can travel through several food-web links, and different species may respond differently.

## Learn It

### Separate identity from impact

Unfamiliar appearance does not establish that a species is non-native or harmful. Reliable identification, local context, and impact evidence matter.

A species native in one region can be introduced elsewhere. A large population alone does not establish invasive status. This lesson uses hypothetical cases so that the reasoning depends on the stated evidence.

### Follow direct and indirect effects

An introduced herbivore can directly consume a native plant. If native insects depend on that plant, they may lose food. Birds eating those insects may then be affected indirectly.

A dense surface plant can shade submerged plants. A filter feeder can reduce suspended food available to native feeders. Harm need not involve directly eating another organism.

### Expect conditions to matter

Temperature, resources, predators, competitors, and other conditions influence whether an introduction persists or spreads. Success in one lake does not guarantee success in another.

A benefit to one native predator does not rule out wider harm. Evaluate several organisms and functions rather than declaring the whole ecosystem helped or harmed from one count.

## Build The Model

For an introduced herbivore, trace:

`more feeding on native plants -> fewer host plants -> fewer dependent insects -> less food for birds`

For an introduced competitor, trace:

`shared food consumed -> less food for native fish -> possible lower native growth`

Food-web arrows usually point from food to consumer. An arrow from native insects to an introduced fish means the fish eats the insects.

## Memory Anchors

- **Origin and impact are different questions.**
- **Follow the links beyond the newcomer.**
- **Count recovery as well as removal.**

## Evidence Rules

Use comparable sampling effort, baseline data, and similar reference sites. Timing alone does not establish cause.

| Site type | Native plants before | Native plants after drought | Loss |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Invaded | 100 | 70 | 30 |
| Uninvaded reference | 100 | 80 | 20 |

The invaded sites lost 10 more plants. Some change may reflect shared drought conditions. The difference suggests a question to investigate; it does not prove the introduced species caused every loss.

For management, measure target reduction, native recovery, and unintended effects. Removing an invader can leave bare soil, missing native plants, or sources of reinvasion nearby. One survey finding none does not guarantee permanent absence.

## Common Traps

- "Every non-native species is invasive." Origin alone is insufficient.
- "A benefit to one species proves no harm." Effects differ across a food web.
- "A decline after arrival proves its cause." Weather and other changes may also contribute.
- "Removing the target restores everything instantly." Habitat and native populations may need time or additional support.

## Pause And Think

Without looking back:

1. What distinguishes non-native status from invasive status?
2. How can a plant affect birds without touching or feeding on birds?
3. Why compare treated sites with untreated sites during the same period?

Check: include harm or likely harm, an indirect feeding link, and shared environmental changes.

## Apply It

A removal project reduces invasive cover from 70% to 20%. Native cover rises from 15% to 25%.

Report both changes: invasive cover fell 50 percentage points, and native cover rose 10 percentage points. The target remains present, and native cover has only partly increased. Continue monitoring soil condition, other organisms, and new arrivals.

Choosing a control organism or removal method requires knowledge of non-target effects. Students should analyze evidence, not release organisms or remove unfamiliar plants themselves.

## Try At Home

**Paper food-web investigation.** Allow 20 minutes. Use paper and a pencil. Draw five cards: native plant, native insect, native insect-eating bird, introduced plant-eating organism, and sunlight.

1. Draw sunlight to plant as an energy-input arrow. Draw food-to-consumer arrows from plant to native insect and from insect to bird.
2. Add an arrow from the plant to the introduced plant-eater.
3. Predict what could happen if the new consumer greatly reduces the plant supply.
4. Add a second plant species that the native insect can also eat. Compare your prediction with the first model.
5. Record an observation that would make you revise each prediction.

| Model | Predicted insect response | Predicted bird response | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| One host plant | | | |
| Two usable host plants | | | |

The model's second food source may reduce dependence on the first, but its quantity and accessibility still matter.

**Explain:** Which arrow changed your reasoning? These are conditional predictions, not measurements of a real invasion. Do not collect, transport, release, or remove organisms for this activity.
