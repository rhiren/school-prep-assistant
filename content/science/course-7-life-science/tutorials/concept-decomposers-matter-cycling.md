# Decomposers and Matter Cycling

## Hook

A leaf falls into a damp garden bed. Months later, only its veins remain. Where did the missing part go?

Before reading on, name two possible destinations. "It disappeared" describes what you see, but does not account for its atoms.

## Big Idea

Decomposers, including many fungi and bacteria, obtain materials and chemical energy by breaking down dead material and waste. Matter moves into their bodies and into the nonliving environment. Some nutrients can later be used by plants.

**Atoms move and recombine; they do not vanish.** Energy transfers too, but some disperses as heat. Recycling matter does not recycle all that heat into useful food energy.

## Learn It

### Follow the material

Dead leaves contain carbon compounds and mineral nutrients. Decomposers use some of this material to build their bodies. During respiration, some carbon returns to the air as carbon dioxide. Other substances return to soil or water.

A plant can use carbon dioxide in photosynthesis and take up mineral nutrients through roots. These are different pathways: most carbon in plant sugars comes from carbon dioxide, not soil minerals.

### Separate breakdown from other changes

A leaf can lose measured mass because water evaporates, fragments blow away, dissolved material washes out, or organisms break down its compounds. A smaller leaf alone does not tell you how much each process contributed.

### Define the boundary

If you weigh only leaves, you miss matter in microbes, gases, and drainage. A theoretical system closed to matter keeps the same total matter even as its parts change. Energy can still enter as light or leave as heat. This is a reasoning model, not a project to seal living organisms in a jar.

## Build The Model

Trace these two routes separately:

`leaf carbon -> decomposer -> carbon dioxide -> photosynthesis -> new leaf`

`leaf mineral nutrients -> decomposition -> soil solution -> roots -> plant tissue`

Add an energy arrow from the decomposer to the surroundings labeled **heat**. Do not label that arrow "carbon atoms."

A model has 100 units of tracked carbon:

| Carbon pool | Start | Later |
|---|---:|---:|
| Leaves | 60 | 35 |
| Organisms | 20 | 25 |
| Gas | 20 | ? |

If these are the only pools and no carbon crosses the boundary, the missing value is 40. The total remains 100.

## Evidence Rules

Compare the same leaf type, starting dry mass, duration, and other relevant conditions when testing one factor.

| Treatment | Starting dry mass | Ending dry mass | Loss |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Moist | 20 g | 12 g | 8 g |
| Dry | 20 g | 18 g | 2 g |

The moist samples lost 6 g more. Replicate samples help check whether the difference is consistent. Equal wet masses would be less useful because they may contain different amounts of water. Even dry-mass loss can include washed-out material or escaped fragments.

## Common Traps

- "Decomposers make the matter disappear." Follow it into organisms, soil, water, and gas.
- "All missing leaf mass became soil." Respiration also transfers carbon to the air.
- "Plants get their carbon from minerals." Carbon dioxide supplies most carbon used in plant sugars.
- "Smaller pieces prove decomposition." Breaking something physically is not the same as showing biological breakdown.

## Pause And Think

Close the earlier sections and explain:

1. How could carbon from an old leaf enter a new leaf?
2. Why might a leaf become lighter overnight without much decomposition?
3. Why is heat not a material that roots absorb to build leaves?

Check: your answers should include carbon dioxide and photosynthesis; evaporation; and the distinction between energy and matter.

## Apply It

After a windy week, a garden plot has less litter. A student claims decomposition sped up.

Look outside the plot before accepting that claim. Leaves collected against a fence support transport. To investigate biological breakdown, also consider fragments, dry mass, moisture, and gases. State what you measured separately from what you infer.

## Try At Home

**Outdoor observation: where is leaf matter going?** Allow 10 minutes on each of two or three visits over several weeks. Use a notebook and, optionally, a camera. Ask an adult to choose an accessible, undisturbed leaf-litter patch.

1. Predict whether the visible litter will look different next time.
2. Photograph the same small area from the same position. Do not move or collect the litter.
3. Record the date, recent rain or wind, visible coverage, and whether whole leaves or small fragments dominate.
4. Return to the same spot and compare your records. Look for nearby piles that could indicate wind movement.

**Explain:** Which changes did you observe? What other explanation besides decomposition could fit them? This activity observes litter change; it cannot by itself measure decomposition rate or identify microbes.
