# Water Transport and Transpiration

## Hook

A celery stalk stands in colored water. Later, colored lines appear in its stem and leaves. Meanwhile, a potted plant can lose mass even though no liquid spills out.

Where did the water travel, and how could water leave without visible drops?

**Predict first:** Draw a possible route from soil to air. Leave space to revise it.

## Big Idea

Roots take up water, xylem transports it, and leaves can release it as water vapor. This loss is called **transpiration**. Water transport connects plant structures, cell water balance, and the water cycle.

Remember: **Roots take in. Xylem carries. Leaves exchange.**

## Learn It

### Follow the water

Water enters roots and moves into conducting tissues. **Xylem** carries water and dissolved minerals through stems toward leaves. Evaporation from leaves contributes to a pull on connected water columns. This is a simplified model, not a tiny motor pumping inside every leaf.

In leaves, water evaporates into internal air spaces. Vapor can leave through **stomata**, small pores involved in gas exchange. Water vapor is invisible; absence of droplets does not mean absence of water loss.

The waxy **cuticle** reduces loss through the leaf surface. Dissolved minerals generally remain when water evaporates: they do not leave as water vapor.

### Balance uptake and loss

Water inside cells helps maintain pressure against their walls. This **turgor** helps plant tissues stay firm. If loss exceeds uptake, cells may lose pressure and the plant may wilt. Wilting does not automatically mean all cells are dead.

Wet soil alone does not guarantee enough uptake. Damaged roots may absorb slowly. A plant that wilts at noon may regain firmness later if water loss decreases enough for uptake to catch up.

### A useful tradeoff

Closing stomata can reduce water loss, but it can also reduce carbon dioxide entry. Carbon dioxide is a matter input for photosynthesis. A water-saving response can therefore limit sugar production.

Drier air and moving air can favor faster water loss under similar conditions. But responses depend on humidity, temperature, water supply, and stomatal opening together.

**Pause:** Explain why a plant might wilt in wet soil. Then check: uptake through damaged roots could still be slower than loss.

## Build The Model

`soil water -> roots -> xylem -> leaf water -> vapor in air`

Add two side notes:

- Leaf evaporation can help pull water upward.
- Stomatal opening affects both water-vapor loss and carbon dioxide entry.

Do not label xylem as the main sugar-distribution tissue. **Phloem** transports sugars. Not every drop taken up is used directly in photosynthesis; water also supports cells and can be lost through transpiration.

| Covered-soil setup | Loss over 2 hours | Average loss rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| Plant in still air | 6 g | 3 g/hour |
| Matched plant with gentle airflow | 10 g | 5 g/hour |

Divide loss by time. The airflow setup lost 4 g more over the full interval, or 2 g/hour more on average. Those are different quantities.

This comparison supports greater measured loss with airflow **in these conditions**. It does not prove the same response for every plant.

## Evidence Rules

A pot's mass can fall because of leaf-associated water loss, soil evaporation, or drainage. Covering the soil reduces one alternative; collecting drainage addresses another. A comparable no-plant setup helps estimate background loss.

Different leaf areas also complicate comparisons. A plant losing 12 g from 200 square centimeters loses 0.06 g per square centimeter. Another losing 9 g from 100 square centimeters loses 0.09. The first loses more total water; the second loses more per unit area over the same time.

Dye shows where colored water traveled. Its visible distance alone does not tell you an exact volume lost per hour.

## Common Traps

- **"No drops means no loss."** Vapor is invisible.
- **"Every gram lost is transpiration."** Check drainage and soil evaporation.
- **"Wilting proves death."** Reduced turgor can be reversible.
- **"More uptake means exactly that much photosynthesis."** Water has several roles.
- **"Dry air always wins."** Stomatal regulation can change the response.

## Apply It

Two plants lose 11 g and 7 g in equal times. The first sits near a fan, but its soil is uncovered; the second's soil is covered.

Can you conclude that the fan caused 4 g of extra transpiration?

**Explain before checking:** No. Different soil evaporation is an alternative explanation. Use similar soil covers, leaf areas, temperatures, starting water conditions, and time intervals. Repeat with matched plants. Even then, describe the measured result within those conditions.

## Try At Home

### Trace a water pathway with celery

Ask an adult to cut the base of two similar leafy celery stalks. Use stable cups on a tray, equal amounts of water, and a few drops of household food coloring in one cup. Leave the other plain. Keep both in the same moderate indoor conditions.

1. Predict where color will first appear.
2. Sketch or photograph each stalk at the start, after an hour, and later that day.
3. Record where you see color, not just whether the whole stalk looks darker.
4. Ask an adult to cut a cross-section if you want to inspect colored conducting regions.

Use only food coloring, avoid eyes and stains, and wash hands and surfaces. Do not eat the experiment materials. Adults handle cutting; discard materials after the observation.

**Evidence limit:** This traces movement in a cut stem without roots. It does not directly demonstrate root uptake, measure exact transpiration, or prove the dye became sugar. No visible color yet is not proof that no water moved.
